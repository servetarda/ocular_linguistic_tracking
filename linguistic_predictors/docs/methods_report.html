Methods report

German Surprisal Pipeline

Audit, correction and validation of linguistic_predictors23–24 September 2026Units: bits (log₂)

1Summary

The pipeline computes word-by-word predictability of a German spoken story at five levels: lexical, part-of-speech, semantic, phonemic and syllabic. It follows Heilbron et al. (2022), with the syllabic level as an extension. I audited the original implementation by running every component against the real models and checking each against a brute-force reference. The audit found errors at every level. The corrected pipeline now reads the text as written, and it defines word probability correctly for sub-word language models. It scores the entire 143,248-word pronunciation dictionary at every position, with a proven error bound. It also handles German-specific spelling, syllable and compound structure.

The language model was chosen by its fit to German eye-tracking data (PoTeC). For the same model, the corrections alone raised the held-out fit of surprisal to human reading times 2.7–3.6-fold. The selected model (GerPT2) fits reading times about twice as well as the originally used one. The corrected pipeline reproduces the expected direction in 13 of 13 behavioural checks, including semantic incongruity, cloze predictability and word-class violations.

2Pipeline and data

For every unit of the text (a word, or a punctuation mark), the pipeline returns probability, surprisal and entropy at each level. Phoneme- and syllable-level values are also returned as one row per phoneme or syllable, for use as mTRF regressors once onset times come from forced alignment.

Stimulus text

Story part 1 was extracted from the provided PDF (2 pages, 481 whitespace tokens, 552 units including punctuation). The PDF's font encoded every lowercase a as the IPA letter ɑ (U+0251), 149 occurrences. These were mapped back to a. The PDF's line wrapping, which falls mid-sentence, was joined into running text. No other characters were altered. The text contains words that appear to be deliberate semantic violations (zu einer dieser Unkraut; litt unter Zeitung; vereinbarte einen Anstrichs). They were left untouched.

3Predictor methods

This section describes the pipeline as it now stands, in the form a methods section would need. Section 4 lists what was wrong before.

3.1 Text units and spelling

Units are maximal letter runs and single punctuation marks ([^\W\d_]+|[.,!?;:]). Digits, quotes and dashes remain in the text the language model reads, but receive no row. Whitespace is normalised (runs of spaces collapsed; at most one blank line).

Spelling seen by the models. The stimulus uses Swiss orthography (ss for ß). For each word containing ss, the ß variant is substituted when it is at least 5 times more frequent in SUBTLEX-DE and attested at least 3 times. This rule converts Äusserst, regelmässig, sass, geniessen, ausserdem and keeps dass, Masse, Fitnessstudio. The number of units never changes. The output column word keeps the original spelling (it is what a forced-alignment TextGrid is matched against); word_model shows what the models read.

Dictionary spelling. The pronunciation dictionary contains no ß; every ß is written ss. Each lexical item is therefore keyed by its dictionary spelling (lowercase, ß → ss). Lookups, G2P and cohort membership all use this key.

3.2 Lexical predictions

Input. GerPT2 reads the text exactly as written: punctuation attached, digits and quotes kept. It is preceded by a document-start seed: the tokenizer's BOS token if the model can embed it, otherwise a newline (GerPT2 embeds its BOS token, <|endoftext|>; the older dbmdz model gets the newline). Model tokens are assigned to units by character offsets. When a text exceeds the 1,024-token window, the context is rebuilt at a sentence start, preceded by the seed, keeping ≈800 tokens. This follows Heilbron et al.'s rule of ≥700 tokens of overlap and no mid-sentence starts.

Word probability. GPT-2's tokens carry the space before a word, so the product of a word's token probabilities is the probability that the next word begins with that string. It is converted into the probability of the word itself (Pimentel & Meister, 2024), where B is the set of tokens that start a new unit (space-, newline- or punctuation-initial):

(1)P(w | c) = P(t1…tn | c) · P(B | c, t1…tn) ⁄ P(B | c)

The denominator applies only when a word ends immediately before the unit. For a space-led word, the choice between a space and a line break is formatting that a listener never hears. It is conditioned away by multiplying by [P(space) + P(newline)] ⁄ P(space), each being the total mass of tokens beginning with that character.

Spoken-word probability. An additional column, lexical_surprisal_spoken, sums every spelling of the word a listener cannot distinguish: its casings, and, for German numerals, the digit string (hundert ≡ 100; parser for cardinals below 10,000, articles excluded).

(2)Pspoken(w | c) = Σv ∈ V(w) P(v | c)    surprisal = −log₂ Pspoken

The main column, lexical_surprisal, is the exact written form, as in the paper. The uncorrected token product is reported as lexical_surprisal_raw.

Entropy. Three measures are reported:

  • lexical_entropy: over the next-word distribution restricted to word-initial tokens and truncated to the nucleus. The nucleus is the smallest set reaching cumulative probability 0.9, with at least 40 items, as specified in the paper's SI A.3.
  • entropy_full: over the untruncated next-token distribution.
  • entropy_words: over the full scored vocabulary (§3.3), with casings merged.

3.3 The candidate distribution

At every position, the probability of every candidate word is computed with Eq. 1. The candidate forms are:

  • all 143,248 dictionary words in lowercase and capitalised form;
  • the standard-spelling (ß) form of the 751 dictionary words where that spelling is standard;
  • the text's own spellings, digit spellings of numerals, and the six punctuation marks.

That is ≈288,000 forms. All forms of an item are summed into the item's probability. This distribution feeds the syntactic, semantic, phonemic and syllabic levels.

Scoring this exactly at every position was made tractable in three ways:

  1. The context's key/value cache is computed once per position. Each candidate runs only its own tokens against it, and the cache is broadcast inside attention rather than copied.
  2. Candidates are arranged in a token trie: 712,009 tokens collapse to 366,064 shared prefixes.
  3. A branch-and-bound search skips subtrees whose total probability is provably negligible. Under Eq. 1, the words below a prefix are disjoint events, so their total probability is at most the prefix probability. A subtree is skipped only while the skipped mass stays below ε = 0.01 of (a) the presented word's own probability, for subtrees containing words that share its first phoneme, or (b) the scored dictionary mass, otherwise. Every cohort probability is therefore within 1% relative error, i.e. under 0.015 bits of surprisal.

3.4 Syntactic (POS) predictions

Each candidate form in the nucleus of the candidate distribution (top-p 0.9, at least 40 forms) is inserted after the preceding words of its sentence and tagged by spaCy (.pos_, Universal Dependencies). Following the paper's SI (A.3), candidates are tagged with left context only ("the context plus the predicted word"). The presented word is tagged within its full sentence. The spaCy document is built from the pipeline's own tokens, so the target index is exact, and the parser, NER and lemmatizer are disabled (tags are unchanged). With Q the nucleus forms sharing the presented word's tag and K the nucleus:

(3)POS surprisal = −log₂ [ Σq ∈ Q P(q | c) ⁄ Σk ∈ K P(k | c) ]

Punctuation rows receive lexical values only, since they have no acoustic onset (--score-punctuation adds the rest).

3.5 Semantic predictions

The predicted meaning is the probability-weighted mean fastText vector of the word forms in the nucleus, renormalised. The prediction error is the cosine distance between that mean and the presented word's vector. It is computed for content words (NOUN, VERB, ADJ, ADV, PROPN). Vectors are looked up in the written casing first; German vectors are case-specific. They come from the cc.de.300 model, cached for the needed forms. Semantic distance, the integration-difficulty control, follows Broderick et al. (2018): 1 − Pearson r between the word's vector and the mean vector of the preceding words in the same sentence (the previous sentence for a sentence-initial word), with punctuation excluded.

3.6 Phonemic predictions

Pronunciations come from MFA's german_mfa dictionary. Unlisted words are transcribed by MFA's German G2P model (20 words in story part 1). The dictionary's meta-entry [bracketed] is excluded. The cohort at position i is the set of words consistent with the first i phonemes heard, including words exactly i phonemes long. Those contribute an explicit end-of-word outcome, as in the paper's Eq. 8 and in Brodbeck et al. (2018):

(4)P(φi = a | φ<i) = Σw ∈ Ci, wi = a p(w) ⁄ Σw ∈ Ci p(w)

In the hierarchical model, p(w) is the GerPT2 item probability from §3.3 over the whole dictionary. The paper used a nucleus with a flat pseudocount tail, so this is more exact. In the single-level model, p(w) is the SUBTLEX-DE count + 1 of every dictionary word. Surprisal and entropy are reported per phoneme.

3.7 Syllabic predictions (extension)

Syllables are built from the same pronunciations, then fed to the same cohort computation (Eq. 4 over syllables). The syllabification rules are:

  • Nuclei. Nuclei are vowels, diphthongs and syllabic consonants (n̩ l̩ m̩). The r-glide ɐ after a monophthong belongs to that vowel (veːɐ.dən), but after a diphthong it is its own nucleus (fɔʏ.ɐ).
  • Onsets. Consonants between nuclei go to the following syllable as far as they form a legal onset. A legal onset is a cluster attested word-initially in at least 10 dictionary entries (53 clusters). Legality is judged on phonemes, not allophones: tʰ = t, c = k, ɟ = ɡ, and vocalised ɐ = ʁ in matching. Loanword-only onsets (/s/ + consonant, /ps/, /ks/) are excluded word-internally. Examples: ɡaʁ.tʰn̩, hʊn.dɐt, fɛns.tɐ, mʊs.kəl.
  • Compound and prefix boundaries. These are hard syllable edges, because German does not resyllabify across them (ʃtʁoːm.an.ʃlʊs, ʃtand.ɔʁt). A word is split into dictionary words, optionally with a linking element, when two conditions hold. First, the parts' geometric-mean SUBTLEX frequency exceeds the word's (Koehn & Knight, 2003). Second, the second part's pronunciation matches the end of the word's pronunciation, and any linking element is actually pronounced (a linking s must be [s]). Splitting recurses, and numerals split around und. 56,223 dictionary words were split.

3.8 Frequency

Unigram surprisal = −log₂ [(count + 1) ⁄ (N + V)], using SUBTLEX-DE counts summed over casings under the standard spelling. N = 25.0 M tokens and V = 190,500 types. The flag in_subtlex marks words the corpus never contains (these receive ≈24.6 bits).

5Validation

5.1 Implementation against brute-force references

  • The shared-cache attention matched the standard Hugging Face forward pass to within 2 × 10⁻⁴ nats. So did incremental caching and the batched candidate scorer.
  • The single-pass text scorer agreed with an independent per-word scorer on every unit (≤ 10⁻⁴ nats).
  • Pruned scoring vs exact scoring over a 20,000-word vocabulary: maximum difference 0.0099 bits in phoneme surprisal and 0.023 bits in entropy across tested positions.
  • Test suites (tests/run_all.py): three offline suites and one real-model suite. All pass. A refactor of the code was checked against a reference run: identical output to within 10⁻¹⁵.

5.2 Language-model selection on human reading data

Data. The Potsdam Textbook Corpus (PoTeC; Jakobi et al., 2025): 75 native German readers, 12 texts, eye-tracking. Per word, first-pass reading time (FPRT) and total fixation time (TFT) were log-transformed and averaged over readers, using fixated words only. Words at screen-line edges, and words with digits, symbols, abbreviations or hyphens, were excluded. The same 933 words entered every model.

Measure. Ordinary least squares on the word means. The baseline contains word length and SUBTLEX-DE Zipf frequency of the current and previous word. The full model adds surprisal of the current and previous word. ΔLL is the held-out log-likelihood gain per word, over 10 repetitions of 10-fold cross-validation, in milli-nats per word. Perplexity is bits per word on the story.

Switching the end-of-word correction off changes ΔLL by less than 2.5 (slightly higher without it) for every model except stefan-it, where the correction adds +9 (FPRT) and +18 (TFT). The correction's main effect is on the cohort, not on the word-level fit.

GerPT2 was selected: it has the best fit to human reading times and runs at ≈9 s per word with the full vocabulary. The largest model had the lowest perplexity but a weaker fit, consistent with Oh & Schuler (2023). Its runtime (≈50 s per word for GerPT2-large; more for XL) also argued against the larger models. GerPT2-large surprisal and entropy are included as comparison columns, so the choice can be checked against the EEG data themselves.

6Limitations and open items

  • Tagger repair. POS surprisal cannot detect an open-class category violation when the tagger re-tags the odd word to fit its slot: spaCy tags einen laufen Mantel as ADJ. Closed-class violations are detected. This is inherent to tagger-based POS surprisal, the paper's method.
  • Model choice rests on reading, not listening. PoTeC is expository text read silently. The choice should be confirmed on the study's own EEG/ocular data using the comparison columns.
  • Heuristic components. Compound splitting and the Swiss-spelling rule are frequency-based heuristics. Syllabification is not morphology-aware beyond compounds and prefixes. 20 story words rely on G2P transcriptions. The dictionary has occasional idiosyncrasies (e.g. sass with a short vowel).
  • Deviations from the paper, each switchable: end-of-word correction (--no-eow-correction), full-vocabulary cohort prior, and the syllabic level itself, which is an extension.
  • Stimulus text. A few spellings may be transcription artifacts (sechs und fünfzig, Bio gemüse, lowercase quadratmeter). The text, the audio and the forced-alignment transcript should agree before alignment.

Not yet produced: the final predictor tables for story part 1. The full run was stopped at the last step (552 of 552 units scored, comparison columns pending) at the user's request. Expected runtime is ≈1 hour on the M4, or ≈15 minutes with --vocab-size 20000.
